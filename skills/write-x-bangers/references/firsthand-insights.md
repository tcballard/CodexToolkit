# Firsthand insights and subject strategy

## When to use

Use for lessons from actual work, a requested series, or ongoing audience positioning. Keep ordinary launches, questions, humour, personal updates and longer reflections available. Do not silently convert every banger into a lesson.

## Find the observation

Start from what happened, not a predetermined moral. Look for a result that changed the author's mind, a failure that exposed an assumption, a surprising constraint, or a trade-off a reader could use. Ask: “What did doing this teach me that someone who has not done it might miss?”

Select one observation. Supply enough concrete evidence to make it credible and preserve uncertainty. Distinguish “this happened to me” from a general causal claim. Do not invent a failed attempt, measurement, emotional reaction, user response or before/after improvement. If the evidence only supports a status update, write that instead.

When it fits, aim for two to four sentences: the observation, a detail from the work, and its practical consequence. These are possible jobs for sentences, not a mandatory order or template. Follow the requested character limit and length-validation reference. Do not add suspense, a question or a moral to fill the shape.

Example facts supplied by Tom: problems get past the building agent more often than a fresh review agent; he now separates build and review sessions. An eligible post can explain that experience and decision. It cannot claim a measured defect reduction, independent mathematical verification, or universal superiority of a second model without evidence.

## Build a recognisable body of work

For a series, infer a subject where the author has continuing firsthand evidence and readers have a reason to return. Define the reader's benefit in plain language. For Tom, start with lessons from building and maintaining real software with agents, drawing mainly on Omarchy, verification, releases and hardware experiments. This is a flexible editorial direction, not a restriction on his interests.

Vary the actual discoveries: debugging, maintenance, review, design, compatibility, release decisions, costs and user feedback. Different project names do not make repeated premises distinct. Keep a mix of useful insights and posts whose purpose is announcement, conversation, humour or personal reflection. Do not prescribe percentages without a brief or evidence.

Treat daily publication and a six-month horizon as optional practice. Skip a weak lesson rather than manufacture one to satisfy a streak. Do not create or change automations or publish anything without authorization. Measure useful conversation, relevant follows and outcomes alongside reach, using the performance-learning reference and consistent observation windows. Do not infer success from one viral example.
